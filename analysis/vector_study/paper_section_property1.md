> - **供给容量（supply）** = 注入深度 × 干预秩（= intro 里的 depth + dimensionality）

\subsection{Experimental Setting}
\label{Experimental Setting}
\textbf{Models and Datasets.} We select Qwen2.5-1.5B-Deepseek, Qwen2.5-7B-Deepseek \citep{guo2025deepseek}, and Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-14B \cite{qwen3} as our base models. The training data spans four topics: mathematical reasoning \citep{yu2025dapoopensourcellmreinforcement}, scientific reasoning \citep{feng2024sciknoweval}, code generation \cite{cui2025process}, and instruction following \citep{zhao2024wildchat1mchatgptinteraction}. For each task, we first train RL teacher models using GRPO/DAPO, then transfer capabilities to student models via both On-policy and Off-policy distillation \cite{li2026rethinking}. On this basis, we compare three update configurations—full fine-tuning, LoRA \citep{hu2021loralowrankadaptationlarge}, and vector steering—to investigate the limits and conditions of low-dimensional compressibility of RL information in activation space. The implementation of vector steering is as follows.

\textbf{Vector Steering.} Given a frozen pretrained model with decoder layers $\{f_\ell\}_{\ell=1}^{L}$ and hidden size $d$, vector steering injects a small set of trainable vectors into the output of selected layers while keeping all original weights fixed. Let $\mathbf{h}_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the output hidden state of layer $\ell$ at a given token position. We attach a steering module to a chosen set of layers $\mathcal{S}\subseteq\{1,\dots,L\}$, and for each $\ell \in \mathcal{S}$ apply
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_\ell' = \mathbf{h}_\ell + \boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell,
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ is the injected steering delta. The vectors $\{\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell\}_{\ell \in \mathcal{S}}$ are the only trainable parameters, learned end-to-end from the distillation signal while the backbone remains fully frozen. This makes vector steering a minimal, low-noise probe that isolates RL's influence in activation space and attributes it to a small set of low-dimensional directions.


\subsection{Effective Manifold Capacity}
\label{Effective Manifold Capacity}

We first establish the central property of this work: RL-induced updates occupy a \emph{low-dimensional effective manifold} in activation space, but whether a given low-dimensional intervention can reproduce them is governed by a matching between the \emph{capacity it supplies} and the \emph{capacity the target demands}. The supplied capacity is set by two factors we vary explicitly—the depth at which the intervention is injected and the rank of the injected correction—while the demanded capacity is set by how far the teacher lies from the student. We term this property \textbf{Effective Manifold Capacity}, and the remainder of this section characterizes it from both the demand and the supply side.

\textbf{RL Updates Are Intrinsically Low-Dimensional.} We first examine distillation performance on the training set (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-recover}(a)). Full fine-tuning and LoRA achieve near-identical results across all datasets, indicating that constraining the update to a low-rank subspace does not significantly impair distillation quality. Pushing compression to its limit, we retain only a single trainable steering vector per layer \citep{braun2025understandingunreliabilitysteeringvectors}. Despite reducing the trainable parameters by several further orders of magnitude relative to LoRA, this single vector still recovers over 85\% of full fine-tuning's gain over the base model on most tasks, and on Qwen3-8B Code it matches full fine-tuning almost exactly. The primary capability RL confers can thus be characterized by a near-one-dimensional direction in activation space—that is, the effective manifold has very low intrinsic dimension.

\textbf{The Effective Direction Is Sampling-Invariant.} We next ask whether this low-dimensional structure is an artifact of on-policy sampling \citep{YanHeLiuJin26}. Switching to off-policy distillation—where the training data are generated by the teacher rather than the student, substantially altering the training distribution—single-vector steering remains nearly as effective, still recovering over 85\% of the full fine-tuning gain on most tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-recover}(b)); the same trend holds on the validation set, showing that this extremely low-dimensional parameterization generalizes rather than merely fitting the training samples. Regardless of whether the data originate from the student or the teacher, the single vector points to the same dominant, reasoning-related direction: the sampling strategy alters only the data distribution, not the effective manifold itself. This isolates the manifold as a stable property of the teacher, independent of how data are drawn, and lets us next probe what \emph{does} change it.

\textbf{Demand Side: Distribution Distance Sets the Required Capacity.} We now vary the teacher while fixing student sampling. Only RL-trained teachers are captured by a single vector; for non-RL teachers, steering degrades sharply or collapses (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-recover}(c)). This echoes a known property of on-policy distillation—only teachers close to the student distribute effectively \citep{yin2026h}—but the constraint is markedly sharper at the vector level: a single vector captures precisely the low-capacity signal characteristic of RL-trained teachers \citep{cai2026learning}. To quantify this, we compare the three configurations in the off-policy setting across teachers at increasing distances from the student (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-recover}(d)). For nearby RL teachers the three perform comparably; as the teacher moves away, a consistent ordering emerges—full fine-tuning $>$ LoRA $>$ vector steering—with the gap widening with distance. Since this ordering mirrors the expressive capacity of the three configurations, it shows that a larger distribution distance \emph{demands} a higher update capacity, and the single vector, supplying the least, is the first to fall short. The demanded capacity is thus governed by distribution distance, not by parameter count per se.

\textbf{Takeaway (Effective Manifold Capacity).} Two conditions must jointly hold for single-vector steering to succeed: the teacher must be RL-trained, and it must be close to the student. The first reflects that the RL signal itself is intrinsically low-dimensional \citep{tiwari2025iclr-geometry}; the second, that reproducing it requires a capacity that grows with distribution distance \citep{wang2026explore}. A low-dimensional intervention succeeds only when the capacity it supplies meets the capacity the target demands—the essence of Effective Manifold Capacity. In the next section we turn to the \emph{supply} side and show that injection depth and intervention rank are the two levers that control it.

\subsection{Supply Side: Depth and Rank Control the Available Capacity}
\label{Supply Side}

The previous subsection showed that the demanded capacity grows with distribution distance. We now fix the target and study the two factors that determine the capacity an intervention can \emph{supply}: the depth at which it is injected and the rank of the correction. This directly instantiates the depth-and-dimensionality dependence stated in Property~1.

\textbf{Depth Controls Supplied Capacity.} We select layers across different depths and inject full fine-tuning, LoRA, or single-vector steering at each (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-depth}(a)). Full fine-tuning and LoRA perform uniformly well at all depths, showing that these higher-capacity configurations can realize the required update anywhere. Single-vector steering, in contrast, shows a clear positional dependence: it is highly effective at shallow-to-intermediate layers but degrades markedly at deep layers. The effectiveness of a low-dimensional intervention is therefore not intrinsic to the direction; it depends on the depth at which the direction is injected. This raises two questions: does the deep-layer failure stem from optimization difficulty or from insufficient expressiveness, and what structural property of the single vector limits its supplied capacity?

\textbf{The Bottleneck Is Expressiveness, Not Optimization.} To separate the two, we examine the training dynamics across depths. Figure~\ref{fig:emc-depth}(b) shows that the gradient magnitude of the steering vector peaks at intermediate layers and decreases toward both ends; crucially, shallow and deep layers receive comparable gradients yet reach starkly different outcomes, so gradient scale alone does not determine effectiveness. Figure~\ref{fig:emc-depth}(c) tracks performance over training: shallow layers, despite small gradients, improve steadily and converge to the level of intermediate layers, whereas deep layers saturate early at a much lower level and show no further improvement under extended optimization. Shallow layers are thus merely slower to converge, while deep layers face a genuine ceiling. This rules out an attenuated optimization signal and localizes the deep-layer bottleneck in expressiveness: a single additive direction near the output is followed by too few nonlinear transformations to be unfolded into the required behavioral change.

\textbf{The Structural Cause Is Input-Invariance.} We identify the concrete source of this expressiveness gap in a structural difference among the configurations. Full fine-tuning and LoRA modify the layer's weight matrices, so the increment they add to the hidden state is a function of the input—each token receives a distinct correction. Single-vector steering instead adds an identical offset $\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell$ to every token, independent of content. At shallow and intermediate layers, the remaining downstream transformations can still convert this constant offset into input-dependent behavior; near the output, too few transformations remain, and an input-invariant shift cannot be reshaped into the input-conditioned correction the task requires. The deep-layer bottleneck is therefore not the low rank of the intervention per se, but its \emph{input-invariance}. This predicts a targeted remedy—rendering the correction input-conditioned while preserving its low-dimensional structure—which is precisely a controlled increase of supplied capacity along the rank axis:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_\ell' = \mathbf{h}_\ell + \boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell(\mathbf{h}_\ell), \qquad
\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell(\mathbf{h}_\ell) = \mathbf{B}_\ell \, \sigma(\mathbf{A}_\ell \mathbf{h}_\ell),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d}$, $\mathbf{B}_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $r \ll d$, and $\sigma$ is a nonlinearity. $\mathbf{B}_\ell$ is zero-initialized so training starts from the unmodified model and gradually learns an input-dependent correction, adding only $O(rd)$ parameters. Because this changes only the input-conditioning of the intervention—not its injection depth—it isolates rank as the controlled variable.

\textbf{Rank Restores Capacity at Deep Layers.} Increasing rank substantially improves deep-layer steering (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-rank}(a)): the KL divergence to the teacher drops and accuracy rises correspondingly. Even $r=1$ yields a marked improvement, and as $r$ grows to $8$ performance approaches full fine-tuning (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-rank}(b)). This confirms that the deep-layer bottleneck originates from input-invariance, and that restoring a minimal degree of input-dependent expressiveness—already a rank-1 nonlinear correction—raises the supplied capacity above the demanded level; further rank closes the remaining gap.

\textbf{Gating Behavior Corroborates the Diagnosis.} We finally inspect the gating signal $\sigma(\mathbf{A}_\ell \mathbf{h}_\ell)$ of the rank-1 correction across tokens (Figure~\ref{fig:emc-gate}). At shallow-to-intermediate layers the gating values are smoothly distributed over $[0,1]$: different tokens receive different corrections, and the intervention modulates its intensity by input content. At deep layers the distribution becomes polarized, with values concentrated near $0$ or $1$—a near-binary pattern in which the gate degenerates into a fixed on/off switch rather than a continuous input-dependent modulator. Consistently, the correction's perturbation of the base activations is modest at shallow-to-intermediate layers but substantially larger at deep layers. Deep-layer steering thus fails not from weak corrections but from \emph{improper} ones: an input-invariant offset is amplified into coarse perturbations rather than input-conditioned modulations, because the network near the output no longer provides sufficient transformation space for such conditioning.

\textbf{Summary.} Together, Sections~\ref{Effective Manifold Capacity} and~\ref{Supply Side} establish \textbf{Property 1 (Effective Manifold Capacity)}: RL updates lie on a low-dimensional effective manifold, but a low-dimensional intervention reproduces them only when the capacity it supplies meets the capacity the target demands. The demanded capacity grows with the teacher–student distribution distance; the supplied capacity is jointly set by injection depth—the number of downstream nonlinear transformations available—and by the rank of the correction. Full fine-tuning and LoRA carry ample capacity from the outset and succeed everywhere; a single vector, supplying the least, succeeds only when the demand is low (a close RL teacher) and the supply is high (shallow-to-intermediate layers), and its characteristic failure mode is input-invariance near the output, which a minimal rank-1 input-conditioned correction already alleviates.
